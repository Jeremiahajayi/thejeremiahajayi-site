1. Define the launch

8–6 weeks before

  • Suggested owner: Product marketing
    What good looks like

    What to produce. One page that product, marketing and sales all agree with before anyone writes an asset. If people disagree on it, they will disagree on everything after it.

    A simple structure

    • What we’re launching, in one sentence
    • Who it’s for, and who signs off on buying it
    • The problem, in the customer’s words
    • What they use today, including doing nothing
    • Why ours is different, and the proof
    • What we want the launch to achieve, and how we’ll measure it
    • Owner, date, dependencies and risks

    Common mistake. Writing a feature spec. A brief explains why a buyer should care; the spec explains how it works.

  • Suggested owner: Product marketing
  • Suggested owner: Leadership
  • Suggested owner: Product marketing
    What good looks like

    What to produce. Three to five measures with a baseline and a target, agreed before launch so the results can’t be reinterpreted afterwards.

    A simple structure

    • Choose measures that match the launch’s goal, not every metric you can track
    • Record the baseline: what does a normal month look like?
    • Set a realistic target and a date to check it
    • Say where each number will come from: CRM, product analytics, the sales team

    Common mistake. Measuring only reach: impressions, likes and press mentions. They’re useful signals, but they don’t tell you whether the launch created sales conversations.

  • Suggested owner: Product marketing
    What good looks like

    What to produce. Short notes from 3–5 customer and 2–3 sales conversations, with the exact phrases people used.

    A simple structure

    • Customers: “Walk me through the last time this problem came up.”
    • Customers: “What did you do about it? What did that cost you?”
    • Reps: “Where do deals stall when this comes up?”
    • Reps: “What do buyers compare us with?”

    Common mistake. Pitching the feature during the interview. You’ll hear polite agreement instead of how people actually describe the problem.

  • Suggested owner: Product marketing
    What good looks like

    What to produce. One sentence your whole team could repeat, plus a clear view of what you’re being compared with.

    A simple structure

    • For [target customer] who [problem],
    • [product] is the [category] that [key benefit].
    • Unlike [current alternative], it [main difference].

    Example. For finance teams at multi-entity companies who approve spend in spreadsheets, Fernway (a fictional company) is the spend management platform that routes every request to the right approver. Unlike email and spreadsheets, it keeps a full audit trail.

    Common mistake. Positioning against a competitor buyers aren’t considering. Often the real alternative is a spreadsheet, an agency or doing nothing.

  • Suggested owner: Marketing

2. Build the message and the plan

6–4 weeks before

  • Suggested owner: Product marketing
    What good looks like

    What to produce. A short messaging block that every asset borrows from.

    A simple structure

    • Headline: the outcome for the buyer, not the feature name
    • Three proof points, each backed by evidence: a number, a customer or a demo
    • One use case: a specific customer, situation and result

    Example. Headline: “Approve spend across every entity in one place.” Proof: approvals routed by entity and amount; full audit trail for auditors; set up in a day with existing ERP data. (Fernway, a fictional company.)

    Common mistake. Proof points that are really features with nothing behind them. If you can’t show it, a buyer is unlikely to believe it.

  • Suggested owner: Product marketing
    What good looks like

    What to produce. One shared list of every launch task, with an owner and a due date, working back from launch day.

    A simple structure

    • Columns: task, channel, owner, due date, status, depends on
    • Start from launch day and work backwards
    • Add time for approvals: legal, partners, leadership

    Common mistake. No room for approvals. Partner and legal sign-off are where launch dates usually slip.

3. Enable sales and customer success

3–2 weeks before

  • Suggested owner: Product marketing
    What good looks like

    What to produce. The few things a rep needs to use the launch on a live call this week.

    A simple structure

    • One deck slide: the problem, what’s new, the proof
    • A one-page summary they can send after a call
    • FAQ: the five questions and objections you expect, with answers
    • A three-sentence talk track
    • Which accounts to contact first

    Common mistake. A 20-slide deck nobody opens. If it isn’t short enough to use on a call, it probably won’t be used.

  • Suggested owner: Product marketing
  • Suggested owner: Product marketing
  • Suggested owner: Customer success
  • Suggested owner: Marketing
    What good looks like

    What to produce. Tracking in place before launch, so you can see what the launch did after it.

    A simple structure

    • UTM links for every channel, with one naming pattern (for example utm_campaign=launch-approvals-2027q1)
    • A launch campaign in your CRM that new and influenced opportunities can be tied to
    • Conversion events on the landing page and demo form
    • A “How did you hear about us?” field, since some buyers will come from posts and conversations that tracking can’t see

    Common mistake. Setting up tracking after launch. The first days are often when interest is highest, and that data can’t be recovered.

4. Get ready

Launch week

  • Suggested owner: Product
  • Suggested owner: Product marketing
  • Suggested owner: Founder
    What good looks like

    What to produce. Drafts for the founder and three to five team members, written in each person’s own voice.

    A simple structure

    • Start with the problem or a moment from a customer conversation
    • What changed, and who it’s for
    • One piece of proof
    • A soft next step: a link, a demo or “reply if this is you”

    Common mistake. Everyone posting the same company text. Identical posts read like an ad and tend to get less reach than personal ones.

6. Turn attention into pipeline

2–30 days after

  • Suggested owner: Sales
    What good looks like

    What to produce. A short follow-up plan by signal, so interested people hear from you while the launch is fresh.

    A simple structure

    • Visited pricing or the demo page: personal email from sales within 48 hours
    • Watched the demo or replied: offer a call
    • Existing customers who could use it: customer success shows them
    • Everyone else: add them to your next email or content piece

    Common mistake. A generic “Did you see our launch?” email. Mention what they looked at and why it might matter to them.

  • Suggested owner: Customer success
  • Suggested owner: Product marketing
  • Suggested owner: Product marketing
    What good looks like

    What to produce. A one-page report against the targets you set before launch, separating what you can know now from what needs more time.

    A simple structure

    • At 30 days: engagement, demo requests, opportunities created, sales usage of assets, early adoption
    • At 60–90 days or later: pipeline progression, revenue, retention and sustained adoption
    • For each measure: target, actual, and what you’ll do about the gap

    Common mistake. Calling the launch a success or failure on revenue at 30 days. In B2B, and especially enterprise sales, many deals take longer than that to close.

  • Suggested owner: Marketing
  • Suggested owner: Product marketing
    What good looks like

    What to produce. A 30-minute review with everyone who worked on the launch, ending with named owners and dates.

    A simple structure

    • What worked, and why do we think so?
    • What underperformed, and what does the evidence say?
    • What will we do differently next time, who owns it and by when?

    Common mistake. A retro that ends with observations and no owners. Write the changes into your launch playbook so the next launch is easier.

How to tell whether the launch worked

Different things are knowable at different times. Judge each stage by what it can actually show you.

Before launch

Positioning tested with customers, a target account list, sales ready to use the launch, tracking working, and a baseline for every measure.

Launch week

Qualified engagement, demo requests, customer questions, the first sales conversations, and any technical issues.

30 days after

Opportunities created, sourced or influenced pipeline, how much sales used the assets, and early product adoption.

Later

Pipeline progression, revenue, retention and sustained adoption. With longer sales cycles, 30 days is often too early to judge revenue.

Reading the numbers: a worked example

A launch gets strong social engagement: plenty of likes, comments and shares. But two weeks later, sales has had very few qualified conversations from it. Before deciding the launch “didn’t work”, check:

  • Audience fit. Who engaged? Peers and other marketers, or people who could buy?
  • Messaging. Did the posts explain the buyer’s problem, or mostly celebrate the feature?
  • Call to action. Was there a clear next step for a buyer, and was it easy to take?
  • Lead quality. Did the demo requests that came in match your target customer?
  • Sales follow-up. Did sales contact the engaged accounts quickly, with a relevant message?
  • Measurement gaps. Are conversations happening that tracking can’t see, such as buyers mentioning the launch on calls? A “How did you hear about us?” field helps here.

Each answer points to a different fix: a different audience, sharper messaging, a better offer, or faster follow-up. That’s the point of measuring: to decide what to change next.

Questions people ask

What should a B2B SaaS product launch checklist include?

Six stages: defining the launch (the GTM brief, tier, owner and measures), building the message and plan, enabling sales and customer success, launch-week checks, launch day itself, and the weeks after, when follow-up turns interest into sales conversations. Many checklists stop at launch day, which is one reason launches get attention but few conversations.

How far in advance should you plan a product launch?

As a guide, six to eight weeks for a major launch, three to four weeks for a feature launch, and about a week for a small update. Partner launches and press usually need the most lead time, because other people’s approvals set the pace. If your date is sooner, add it above and the checklist will compress the plan and show which tasks to do first.

What is a launch tier?

A way of sizing the launch effort to the release. Tier 1 is a new product, a major feature that changes the pitch, a partnership or a new market. Tier 2 is a meaningful feature that solves an established customer problem or helps win deals. Tier 3 is an incremental improvement. Tiering stops teams from overspending on small updates or underselling big ones.

Who should own a product launch?

One person accountable for the plan and the date, often product marketing, or a founder at an early-stage company. Product owns what ships; sales and customer success own using it with customers. The owner makes sure all three work to one timeline. The checklist suggests an owner for each task, which you can change.

How do you measure whether a launch worked?

Agree the measures and targets before launch: for example qualified demo requests, opportunities created, sourced and influenced pipeline, sales usage of launch assets and adoption. Review them at 30 days, and again later for results that take longer, such as revenue. Press coverage and social reach are useful signals, but they don’t show on their own whether the launch created sales conversations.
